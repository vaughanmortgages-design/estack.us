Keep business money separate from day one.

Mixing business and personal cash makes taxes, bookkeeping and liability protection harder than they need to be. Here is what to compare before opening a business bank account.

Important: eStack.US is not a bank. Fees, limits and account terms change often, so this page is a comparison checklist, not a ranking. Check the current fee schedule and terms directly with the bank or credit union before opening an account.

Monthly fees and waivers

Check the monthly maintenance fee and exactly what waives it — a minimum balance, a number of debit purchases, or linked accounts. A free account with a balance requirement you cannot hold is not free.

Transaction limits

Many business accounts cap free monthly transactions — deposits, checks, transfers — and charge per item after that. Count your real monthly volume before choosing.

Cash handling

If your business takes cash, check cash-deposit limits and fees, and whether the bank has branches or ATM access near you. Online-only accounts can be a poor fit for cash-heavy businesses.

Minimum opening deposit

Some accounts require an opening deposit and an ongoing minimum balance. Confirm both, plus what happens to fees if the balance dips below the line.

Integrations

Check that the account connects with your accounting software, payment processor and invoicing tools. Clean integrations save hours of manual bookkeeping every month.

Payments in and out

Review ACH and wire costs, mobile check deposit limits, and whether you get a business debit card. If you pay contractors or suppliers by ACH, per-transfer fees add up fast.

Documents you will usually need

Banks verify the business and the people behind it. Requirements vary by state and business type, but commonly include:

For most businesses

Government-issued ID for each owner, your EIN confirmation letter from the IRS (or Social Security number for a sole proprietor), and a business address.

For LLCs and corporations

Articles of organization or incorporation, an operating agreement or corporate governing documents, and any state business license your trade requires.

Open the account in the business's legal name and keep formation paperwork current — mismatched documents are the most common reason an application stalls.

Online bank, credit union or big bank?

Online business banking

Often lower fees and better apps, with limits on cash deposits and no local branch. Good fit for service and e-commerce businesses.

Credit unions and community banks

Local relationships and in-person help, sometimes with lower transaction allowances. Good fit for cash businesses and owners who want a banker who knows them.

National banks

Branches and ATMs everywhere, with fee schedules that reward larger balances. Good fit for businesses operating in several states.

Bottom line

Pick the account whose fee schedule matches how your business actually moves money: count your transactions, check the cash rules, confirm the integrations, and read the waiver conditions. Then check current terms with the institution and open it in the business's name.
