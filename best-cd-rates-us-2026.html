CDs pay you for patience.

A certificate of deposit locks in a rate for a set term. Understand the term, the penalty and the ladder before you lock money away.

Important: eStack.US is not a bank. CD rates and penalties vary by institution and change frequently, so this guide covers how to compare offers rather than ranking today's rates. Check the current APY and terms directly with the bank or credit union.

How a CD works

You deposit a set amount for a fixed term and earn a fixed APY for that term. In exchange for the commitment, CDs often pay more than a regular savings account — but the money is meant to stay put until maturity.

Terms

Common terms run from a few months to five years. Longer terms usually pay more, but they also lock your money through more rate changes. Match the term to a date you actually need the cash.

Early-withdrawal penalties

Breaking a CD early usually costs a set number of months of interest, and on a young CD the penalty can eat into principal. Read the penalty schedule before you deposit, not after.

Maturity and grace periods

When a CD matures, most banks give a short grace period to withdraw or change terms. Miss it and the CD may auto-renew at whatever rate is then on offer — which may be far lower than the one you signed up for.

Deposit insurance

Confirm the CD is issued by an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union and stays within coverage limits. Brokered CDs add extra rules — check how interest is paid and what happens if you sell early.

No-penalty CDs

Some banks offer CDs that allow an early withdrawal without penalty, usually at a lower APY than a standard CD of the same term. They sit between a savings account and a traditional CD.

The CD ladder, explained

A ladder splits your money across several CDs with staggered maturities, so part of it comes due regularly instead of all at once. Example only — not a rate offer: split $10,000 into four $2,500 CDs maturing in one, two, three and four years. Each year one CD matures. You can spend it, or reinvest it at the end of the ladder at current rates. You give up a little of the top long-term rate in exchange for regular access and less regret if rates move.

CD vs high-yield savings account

Choose a CD when

The money has a known future date — a tax bill, a tuition payment, a home purchase — and you want the rate locked in until then.

Choose a savings account when

The money is an emergency fund or might be needed on short notice. Our high-yield savings guide covers APY, fees and minimums.

The rate question

A CD rate is fixed for the term; a savings APY is variable and can drop at any time. If you expect rates to fall, locking in has value. If you expect them to rise, a long lock-in can cost you. Nobody knows — which is exactly why ladders exist.

Taxes

CD interest is generally taxed as income in the year it is credited, even if you leave it in the CD. Factor that into comparisons with other places you park cash.

Bottom line

Compare CDs on four things: the APY, the term, the early-withdrawal penalty and the maturity grace period. Ladder the money you can lock away, keep emergency cash in a savings account, and check current rates with the institution before you commit.
